# Summary

This RFC sets the Rust project's roadmap for 2021. The focus of the year is project health, specifically as it relates to Rust's governance structure. There are three goals:

- Establish a written charter for every team in the Rust project.
- Provide a unified process and vocabulary across the project.
- Create a single place that tracks the projects currently under way.

The core team will drive these efforts, working with every team and working group. The aim is to make our written documentation good enough that new team members can get up to speed without relying on hallway knowledge, and that current team members can quickly work out who to consult about a new idea or project.

# Motivation

When Rust 1.0 shipped there were a handful of teams, and most people involved sat on more than one of them. Information moved between teams because the same people were in the rooms. Nobody needed to write down what each team was responsible for, because everybody already knew.

That is no longer true. The project now has well over a dozen teams and many more working groups and project groups, with hundreds of members. Most people belong to one or two of them. Cross-team conversation no longer happens on its own, and the gaps are showing:

- **Unclear ownership.** It is often not obvious which team has the final say on a question that touches two areas, for example a language feature that needs library support and compiler work. Contributors bounce between teams, or work stalls because each team assumes another one owns the decision.
- **Inconsistent process.** Each team has developed its own way of making decisions, tracking work and recording outcomes. Words like "accepted", "approved", "nominated" and "stabilized" mean slightly different things in different places. A contributor who learns one team's process cannot assume it carries over.
- **Hard to see what is happening.** There is no one place to find the list of ongoing initiatives. Project groups and working groups are created and wound down with little visibility outside their parent team. People start work that duplicates something already under way, or miss a chance to help.
- **Hard to onboard.** New team members learn their team's scope and norms by watching and asking. That works, but it is slow, it depends on someone having time to explain, and it favors people who are comfortable asking.

None of these problems is new, and none of them is urgent on any particular day. That is exactly why they need a roadmap: they will not get fixed as a side effect of other work.

The project has also had a difficult year. The pandemic changed how and when people contribute, and the restructuring at Mozilla affected many contributors directly. A project with clear written structure copes better with people coming and going, because less of its knowledge lives in specific people's heads.

# Guide-level explanation

## Team charters

Every team, working group and project group will have a charter: a short document, kept in a known location, that states:

- the team's **purpose**: the problem it exists to solve;
- its **scope**: the areas it owns and makes decisions about, and the areas it explicitly does not;
- its **parent team** and any **subteams**;
- how it **makes decisions**, including whether it uses the RFC process, FCP, or something lighter;
- how to **contact** it and how its members are chosen.

Charters are written by the teams themselves. The core team will provide a template and review drafts for gaps and overlaps between teams, but it will not dictate content. When two charters claim the same area, or an area is claimed by no one, the teams involved will resolve it, with the core team helping if needed.

## Unified process and vocabulary

We will write down a shared glossary of the terms used across the project's processes, along with a description of the common decision-making mechanisms that teams can adopt. Teams are not required to use identical processes; some differences are healthy. The aim is that when two teams use the same word, it means the same thing, and when they differ, the difference is documented.

Concretely, this includes:

- definitions of the RFC lifecycle states and the FCP dispositions;
- a description of what it means for a team to "own" a decision;
- a standard way to start, run and close a project group or working group.

## Project tracking

We will set up a single, public list of ongoing projects across all teams. Each entry records what the project is, which team it belongs to, who is leading it, and how to get involved. Entries link to the project's own tracking, so this is an index, not a replacement for the tools teams already use.

The list must be cheap to keep current. It will be updated as part of the existing process for creating and closing project groups, rather than through a separate reporting step that will be forgotten.

# Reference-level explanation

The core team will:

1. Publish the charter template and the glossary draft early in the year.
2. Ask each top-level team to complete its charter, and those of its subteams, by the middle of the year.
3. Review the full set of charters for overlaps and gaps, and work with teams to resolve them.
4. Launch the project index and populate it with currently active projects.
5. Report progress publicly each quarter.

Charters and the glossary will live in the `rust-lang/rust-forge` repository, or a successor, so that they are versioned and open to pull requests. Changes to a team's charter require that team's approval. Changes that affect another team's scope also require that team's approval.

# Drawbacks

Governance work takes time from people who might otherwise spend it on technical work. This year's roadmap has no headline language or compiler goals, which may be disappointing to users.

Written charters can become stale or be used to argue about turf. The review process and the ability to amend charters through ordinary pull requests are meant to keep them current and cooperative.

# Rationale and alternatives

We could set a technical roadmap again, as in previous years. Technical work continues regardless, through each team's own plans. What does not happen without explicit focus is the cross-team structural work, which is why it is the theme this year.

We could also attempt a larger governance reform. That would be premature. Charters and shared vocabulary are a prerequisite for any larger change, because they describe how things work today.
