## Summary

The focus of this year is on project health, specifically as it relates to Rust's governance structure.

- Establishing charters for teams in the Rust project
- Provide for unified process and vocabulary across the project
- Creating a single place for tracking a list of ongoing projects

The core team will drive these efforts in coordination with all Rust teams and working groups. As the Rust teams have grown over the years, there is increasingly less cross-chatter that naturally happens due to shared membership of teams, and this RFC aims to improve our written documentation to provide for a smoother onboarding process for new team members, as well as improving the ability of current team members to evaluate who to consult on new ideas or projects.

## Motivation

Rust started as a project with a handful of contributors who knew each other well. Over the following years it has grown into dozens of teams and working groups, with hundreds of regular contributors, spread across time zones and employers. The language, compiler, libraries, tooling, infrastructure, documentation, community and moderation work are all carried out by separate groups, each with its own habits.

When the groups were small, much coordination happened by accident. Someone on the language team was also on the compiler team, and someone on the library team also read the Cargo issue tracker. A question such as "who should look at this?" had an obvious answer because the person asking knew everyone. That is no longer true. Many contributors are on a single team, and know the neighboring teams only by name. Newcomers have to learn by trial and error where a proposal belongs, and a surprising amount of effort goes into questions like "is this a language team matter or a compiler team matter?"

The result shows up in a number of ways:

- Proposals stall because it is unclear which team owns the decision, or because affected teams were not consulted until late.
- Teams use different words for the same thing. "Working group", "project group", "initiative" and "team" are used with overlapping meanings, and what is expected from each is not written down.
- It is hard to see what the project as a whole is working on. Progress is recorded in a mix of issue trackers, blog posts, meeting notes and chat channels, so even people deeply involved in the project cannot easily list the active efforts.
- New team members receive onboarding that depends on whoever happens to mentor them.

None of these is a crisis. The project ships a release every six weeks and has done so for years. But a project of this size runs on shared understanding, and a year of deliberate work on the written record is a better investment than another year of features built on the informal structure.

## Goals

There are three goals for the year. Each has a concrete deliverable and an owner.

### 1. Charters for teams

Every team in the Rust project should have a charter: a short document, kept in the team's repository or in the shared teams repository, which says:

- What the team is for, and the scope of decisions it is responsible for.
- What the team is explicitly not responsible for, and which team to contact instead.
- Who is on the team, how members are added and removed, and how the team's leadership is chosen.
- How the team makes decisions, how it records them, and what happens when it cannot agree.
- How the team interacts with other teams, including who must be consulted on which kinds of changes.

Charters are intentionally brief. The aim is not legalistic detail but a place to point to when a new member asks how things work. The core team will provide a template with the headings above, and each team will fill it in. Teams are free to add sections that matter to them. Existing charters, such as those of the language and library API teams, will be used as examples.

The charters are reviewed in public through pull requests to the teams repository, so that neighboring teams can raise overlaps and gaps. Where two charters claim the same scope, or where an area is claimed by no one, the core team will convene the teams involved to settle it.

### 2. Unified process and vocabulary

The project uses several terms for groups of people and several ways of running a piece of work. We will agree on a small set of terms and define them in one place.

A proposed vocabulary:

- A **team** is a standing group with a charter and long-term responsibility for an area.
- A **working group** is a group with a bounded purpose, including a deliverable, set up by one or more teams and answerable to them.
- A **project group** is the successor to the earlier "initiative" and describes a time-boxed effort to design or implement a particular feature, with a named lead.

The glossary is paired with a description of the typical lifecycle of a feature: idea, discussion, proposal, decision, implementation, stabilization and maintenance, and the teams that normally participate at each stage. Existing processes, such as RFCs, major change proposals, and stabilization reports, are not replaced. They are described in the same place and with the same vocabulary.

Part of this work is to remove duplication. If two processes serve the same purpose for different teams, we will say so and evaluate whether they should be merged. We expect that most differences will turn out to be justified, but they should be justified in writing.

### 3. A single place to track ongoing projects

There should be one list of what the Rust project is working on. Each entry will have a name, a one-paragraph description, the owning team or teams, a lead, a current status, and links to the tracking issue, the design documents and the discussion channel. The list will be published on the project website and maintained by the teams that own the entries, with minimal central curation.

The purpose is partly external. People who want to contribute, or who want to know when a feature might arrive, will have a place to look. It is also for internal use: a team considering a new idea can check what is already under way, and a contributor looking for work can find projects that need help.

The list is not a roadmap in the sense of promised dates. Entries record intent and status, not commitments. Entries that go quiet are marked as such, and may be archived after a notice period.

## Non-goals

This RFC does not change who is on any team, or alter the decision-making authority of any team. It does not set technical priorities for the language, compiler or libraries. Technical roadmaps are produced by the teams responsible, and the intent is that they fit into the common project list. It also does not introduce new bureaucracy for individual contributors. Someone who wants to fix a bug or write a small improvement should not have to know any of this.

## Plan and timeline

The core team will drive the work in coordination with team leads.

- **First quarter.** Publish the charter template and the draft glossary for comment. Collect the list of existing teams and working groups, along with their current descriptions.
- **Second quarter.** Teams write and submit charters. The core team reviews them for overlap and gaps. The glossary is finalized.
- **Third quarter.** Stand up the project list on the website, seed it from existing tracking issues, and ask team leads to review their entries. Document the feature lifecycle.
- **Fourth quarter.** Evaluate the results with team leads, identify what was missing, and decide what should continue into the following year.

Success will be judged by simple observations: do all teams have a charter; can a newcomer find the right team for a question; can a reader get a list of the project's current efforts without searching several places? We will ask for feedback from recent new team members, who are best placed to judge whether the onboarding material helps.

## Drawbacks

Writing charters takes time from people who are already busy. There is a risk that they will become stale documents which nobody reads. To mitigate this, charters will be short, stored next to other team metadata, and revisited when team membership changes.

A shared vocabulary may force some teams to change names they are used to. The costs are small and one-time, but they are real.

A central project list can be seen as a step toward central control. It is meant only as a view of work the teams already do, and the entries are owned by the teams.

## Rationale and alternatives

We could continue as before and let structure evolve organically. The cost is borne most heavily by newcomers and by teams at the edges, and the project has the capacity now to do better.

We could instead create a new body to coordinate between teams. We prefer written shared documents to a new layer of people. The aim is for existing teams to coordinate more easily, not for an intermediary to do it for them.

We could prioritize technical roadmaps, as in earlier years. Language and tooling goals remain important, and teams will continue to pursue them, but this year they do not need a project-wide theme.

## Unresolved questions

Where should the charters live: one repository for all, or one per team? The initial preference is the shared teams repository, to ease discovery.

How strictly should the vocabulary be enforced for existing groups with established names?

How should entries for work that crosses the project boundary, such as efforts led from the foundation or a corporate sponsor, be listed on the project page?
