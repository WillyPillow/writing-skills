# Rust RFC 3037: Project roadmap for 2021 on governance

- Feature Name: not applicable
- Start Date: 2020-11-10
- RFC PR: rust-lang/rfcs#3037
- Rust Issue: not applicable

## Summary

This RFC sets the focus of the Rust project for the coming year on project health, and in particular on the governance structure that coordinates the project's teams. It proposes three concrete efforts. The first is to establish charters for each team in the project. The second is to provide a unified process and shared vocabulary across teams. The third is to create a single place where the project tracks its ongoing initiatives.

The core team will drive these efforts in coordination with every Rust team and working group. The RFC does not propose changes to the language itself, to the release process, or to the stability guarantees of the standard library. Its subject is how the people who maintain the project organize their work.

## Motivation

The Rust project has grown considerably since its early years. Its teams now cover language design, compiler implementation, libraries, tooling, documentation, infrastructure, moderation, and a number of working groups focused on particular domains. Each of these has its own membership, its own rhythm, and its own way of making decisions.

As the teams have grown, the amount of informal cross-team communication has decreased. In the early project, a small group of people participated in nearly every discussion, and new ideas naturally reached the people who needed to hear them. Today many contributors belong to one team and rarely interact with others. Shared membership across teams still exists, but it is no longer enough to keep everyone informed about what other groups are doing.

This has practical consequences. New team members have difficulty understanding what their team is responsible for and how it relates to others. Current members find it hard to decide which other team to consult when they have a new idea or a proposed project. Decisions may be made in one group without the knowledge of another group whose work is affected. The RFC identifies these as symptoms of a single underlying problem: the project's written knowledge about its own structure is incomplete.

The RFC therefore proposes to improve that written knowledge. Better documentation is intended to help new people join, to help existing people find the right collaborators, and to make it easier for the project as a whole to see what it is working on.

## Detailed design

### Team charters

Each team should have a charter that states its purpose, the scope of its responsibilities, the decisions it can make without consulting others, and the decisions that require consultation or approval from another team. The charter should also list the team's current members and the process by which members are added or removed.

The charters are not intended to be legal documents. They are meant to be short, readable, and updated as the team's work changes. The core team will provide a template to make the charters consistent, and each team will be responsible for keeping its own charter current.

### Unified process and vocabulary

The RFC asks the project to agree on a common set of terms for the stages of work that cross team boundaries. Words such as "proposal," "accepted," "stabilization," and "working group" are used throughout the project, but they are not always used with the same meaning. A shared glossary and a description of the common process for moving an idea from a rough sketch to an accepted change would reduce misunderstandings.

The process described in the RFC is deliberately lightweight. It does not replace the existing RFC process, which remains the way that significant changes to the language and libraries are proposed. Instead, it covers the wider set of activities that the teams carry out, such as creating a new working group, retiring an initiative, or handing responsibility from one group to another.

### A single place for tracking projects

The third element is a central list of the ongoing projects across the teams. Each entry would name the project, the teams involved, the people leading it, its current status, and the place where discussion happens. The list would be updated regularly and would be visible to everyone in the project.

The RFC does not prescribe a particular tool for the list. It asks the core team to choose a format that is easy to maintain and easy to search, and it suggests that the list be kept in a public repository so that changes can be reviewed in the same way as code.

## Rationale and alternatives

The RFC considered leaving the project structure as it was and relying on existing channels of communication. The authors judged this insufficient, because the channels that worked in the early project do not scale to the current size. Adding more meetings was also considered, but the authors were concerned that meetings would add to the load on already busy people without solving the problem of written knowledge.

A more ambitious alternative was to restructure the teams themselves, for example by merging some and splitting others. The RFC rejects this for now. Restructuring is a larger decision that depends on the outcome of the charter work, and the authors prefer to understand the current structure before proposing changes to it.

The RFC also considered focusing the year on technical goals, such as a particular feature or performance target. The authors acknowledged that such goals have value, but they argued that the project's most pressing constraint was the difficulty of coordinating work, and that technical progress would be easier once that constraint was reduced.

## Drawbacks

Writing charters and a shared process takes time, and the people who would write them are often the same people who are most needed for day-to-day technical work. There is a risk that the effort will stall, or that the documents will be written once and then forgotten.

There is also a risk of bureaucracy. A project that writes too many processes may discourage contributors who prefer to make changes quickly. The RFC responds that the processes described are intended to be light, and that they exist to make the project's existing practices visible rather than to add new approvals.

## Unresolved questions

Several questions remain open. The first is how the charters should handle decisions that span more than two teams. The second is who has final authority when two teams disagree about the scope of a project. The third is how often the central list should be reviewed, and by whom. The RFC leaves these questions to the core team to resolve in the course of the year, and it asks that the answers be recorded in the project's documentation as they are reached.

## Plan for the year

The RFC sets out a rough schedule. Early in the year, the core team would publish a template for team charters and invite each team to draft one. Midway through the year, the charters would be reviewed in a joint session, and the shared vocabulary would be drafted. Toward the end of the year, the central list would be populated with the projects that are active at that time, and the process would be described in a document that all teams can refer to.

The schedule is a plan and not a commitment. The RFC notes that progress depends on the availability of volunteers, and it asks the community to be patient if some items take longer than expected.

## Conclusion

The project's coordination problems are largely problems of written knowledge. By writing charters, agreeing on a shared process and vocabulary, and tracking ongoing projects in one place, the Rust project can help new members join more easily, help current members find the right collaborators, and make the work of the whole community more visible. The RFC asks the teams to take up these efforts in the coming year, with the core team providing coordination and templates.
