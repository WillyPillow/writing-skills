## Summary

The focus of the Rust project in 2021 is project health, and in particular the documentation of how the project governs itself. The roadmap has three goals:

1. Each Rust team and working group has a charter.
2. The project uses one process and one vocabulary for the work that crosses teams.
3. One place lists the projects that are under way.

The core team will drive this work, in coordination with all Rust teams and working groups.

## Motivation

The Rust project is run by teams and working groups, each responsible for one area: the language, the compiler, the library, Cargo, documentation, and others. [TODO: give the current number of teams, working groups and members, and how they changed over recent years.]

When the project was smaller, many people belonged to several teams. A member of the compiler team who was also on the language team carried knowledge between the two without anyone arranging it. Decisions in one team reached the other through those shared members, and a contributor who wanted to know who owned a question could ask someone who sat on both.

As the teams have grown, that overlap has shrunk. More members belong to a single team, so less information moves between teams by the informal route. The knowledge that used to travel through people is not written down, and two problems follow.

First, a new team member has to learn how their team works by watching it. How decisions are made, which questions belong to the team, and what the team does not own are rarely documented. Second, current members cannot easily tell whom to consult. Someone with an idea that touches several areas has to find out, by asking around, which team owns each part and whether anyone is already working on something similar. Both problems have the same cause, and the same fix: write down what each team does, how teams work together, and what is in progress. That is the scope of this roadmap.

## Goals

### Team charters

Each team and working group writes a charter. A charter says:

- what the team is responsible for, and what it is not;
- how the team makes decisions;
- how someone outside the team brings a question or proposal to it;
- which other teams it depends on or shares areas with.

The goal is met when every team and working group listed on the project's governance page has a published charter that covers these points. [TODO: confirm whether working groups that are expected to end within the year also need charters.]

Writing the charter is the team's job. The core team provides a template and reviews the charters for gaps and overlaps between them. Where two charters claim the same area, or where an area is claimed by no team, the core team raises it with the teams involved.

The charters describe how teams already work. Changing a team's responsibilities is not the purpose of this exercise, though writing them down may show places where a change is needed. [TODO: decide how such changes are handled, for example by a separate RFC.]

### Unified process and vocabulary

Teams currently use different words for the same things, and different steps for the same kind of decision. [TODO: give specific examples, for example different names for a design proposal, or different ways of approving one across teams.] A contributor who works with two teams has to learn both.

The goal is a short shared glossary and a shared description of the steps that cross teams: how a proposal moves from an idea to an accepted plan, how a team signs off on work that affects another team, and how a decision is recorded.

This goal does not require every team to use identical internal processes. Teams differ in size and in the kind of decisions they make, and a process that suits one may not suit another. What should be shared is the interface between teams: the terms and steps that someone moving from one team to another meets.

The goal is met when the glossary and the cross-team process are published, and each team's charter refers to them. [TODO: name the owner of the glossary after it is published.]

### A single list of ongoing projects

Today, projects are tracked in team repositories, tracking issues, meeting notes, and working group pages. [TODO: list the current places, to show the scale of the problem.] To find out whether someone is already working on an idea, a contributor has to search all of them.

The goal is one place that lists every ongoing project in the Rust project, with its owning team, a contact, and its current state. Teams keep their own detailed tracking where they need it; the list links to it.

## How the work will be done

The core team drives the work. For each goal it will:

1. Propose a template or format and publish it for comment.
2. Work with each team to apply it.
3. Review the results across teams for gaps and conflicts.
4. Report progress publicly. [TODO: say how often, and where.]

## Drawbacks

The work takes time from team members who would otherwise spend it on technical work. Teams that are already stretched may find the charter and process work hard to fit in.

Written process can become fixed. A charter that is followed to the letter may slow a team that needs to change how it works. Charters should therefore be easy to amend, and the roadmap should say how. [TODO: say how a charter is amended.]

The goals are about documentation, and documentation does not by itself restore the communication that shared membership used to provide. This roadmap bets that writing things down covers much of the gap. If it does not, the project will need other measures, such as regular cross-team meetings.
