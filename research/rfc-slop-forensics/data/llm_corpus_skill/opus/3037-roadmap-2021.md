## Summary

The focus of this year is project health, and specifically Rust's governance structure. The core team will drive three efforts, in coordination with all Rust teams and working groups:

1. Each team in the Rust project establishes a charter.
2. The project adopts a shared process and a shared vocabulary.
3. The project keeps a list of ongoing projects in a single place.

## Motivation

The Rust project is organized into teams and working groups, each responsible for an area such as the compiler, the language, or the package manager. When there were few teams, many people belonged to several of them. Shared membership carried information between teams without anyone writing it down. A team planning a change could often ask a member who also sat on the affected team.

As the teams have grown, shared membership has become less common, and less of that information travels between teams on its own. New team members have to learn what their team is responsible for and how it works. Much of this knowledge is not written down, so they learn it by asking or by watching. [TODO: author to give an example, such as a recent onboarding where this caused delay or confusion.]

Current team members with a new idea have to find out who to consult. A change to the standard library may need input from the language team, the compiler team, and the documentation team. Without a written statement of each team's responsibilities, the person proposing the change relies on knowing whom to ask. The three efforts in this roadmap address these problems by writing down what is now carried by shared membership: what each team is for, how decisions are made, and what is being worked on.

## Goals

Each goal below says how the project will know it was met.

### Team charters

Every team and working group has a written charter, published in one location that all teams use. A charter states at least:

- what the team is responsible for, and what it is not;
- how the team makes decisions;
- how someone outside the team brings a question or proposal to it.

The goal is met when every team listed in the project's governance records has a published charter. [TODO: author to set the deadline, and to decide whether the core team approves charters or each team approves its own.]

The hardest part of this goal is the boundaries between teams. A charter that lists a team's responsibilities is easy to write in isolation. The difficulty is where two teams both believe an area is theirs, or neither does. Charters written one team at a time will not find these gaps and overlaps. This draft therefore proposes that the core team compare the charters once drafts exist, and resolve conflicts with the teams involved. ### Shared process and vocabulary

Teams use the same names for the same stages of work, and the same steps for common processes. [TODO: author to list the processes in scope, for example how a proposal is accepted, how a team decides by consensus, and how a decision is recorded.] A glossary defines each term.

The goal is met when the glossary is published and each team's charter uses its terms. [TODO: author to decide whether teams that already have their own process must change it, or only describe it in the shared terms.]

The force behind this goal is the person who works with more than one team. If two teams call different stages by the same word, or the same stage by different words, that person cannot tell where a proposal stands. This roadmap does not prescribe a single process for every team. It asks that where teams follow the same process, they describe it the same way.

### A single list of ongoing projects

The project keeps one list of ongoing projects across all teams, in one place. For each project, the list gives at least its owner, the team responsible, and its current stage, in the shared vocabulary.

The goal is met when the list exists, and each team has entered its ongoing projects and keeps them current. [TODO: author to decide where the list lives, who may edit it, and how "current" is checked, for example by a review at a fixed interval.]

This list addresses the second problem in the motivation: a member with a new idea can see who is working on related things before choosing whom to consult.

## Who does the work

The core team drives all three efforts. It coordinates with every team and working group, since only each team can write its own charter and list its own projects.

The cost falls on team members, who will spend time on documents rather than on their usual work. For small teams, writing a charter may take a noticeable share of their time. ## Drawbacks

Documents can go stale. A charter written this year and never updated becomes misleading, which is worse than no charter, because readers trust it. The same holds for the project list. Each goal above needs an owner for upkeep, not only for the first version. [TODO: author to name who keeps each document current.]

A focus on governance means the roadmap sets no direction for technical work this year. Users who read the roadmap to learn what Rust will deliver will not find that here.

Shared vocabulary can conflict with teams' existing practice. A team that has its own working terms must either change them or keep two sets. Both cost effort, and the second risks the confusion the glossary is meant to remove.
